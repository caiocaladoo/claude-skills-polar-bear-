# Point of View Statement

## When To Use
You had opinions in every meeting for twenty years, and none of them is written down under your own name. Inside an employer the firm's view was your view. Use this to get the two or three things you actually believe about your field onto one page, each with the reason it matters now and the work that backs it.

## When Not To Use
If you need one sentence about what you do rather than what you believe, run Positioning Statement. If you cannot yet name a piece of your own work, start with Career Proof Inventory; beliefs with no evidence stay private.

## Inputs
- Your answers to the three questions below, typed or as a pasted transcript of you talking
- Optional: notes where you disagreed with advice in your field, and your Career Proof Inventory
- Optional: memory turned on, so the result sits under a Topic you can edit in Settings > Memory
If you have none of this, I start with the three questions and mark the output as a first draft.

## Approach
Two ideas shape it. Name the shift: the change in the world that makes a belief matter now. And the test The OpEd Project gives for a thesis: it is an argument someone could disagree with. Claude asks and records; it never offers a belief of its own. The failure this prevents is the borrowed opinion, a confident line that sounds like everyone's feed and falls apart the first time a prospect asks "why do you think that?"

## Workflow
1. Ask three questions, in this order, and wait for each answer: what did you tell people at work that surprised them, what common advice in your field do you disagree with, and what has changed in your field that people have not caught up with?
2. From your answers, draft up to three beliefs, each as an arguable thesis in your words. Test each: could a reasonable peer disagree? If nobody would, it is a platitude, and I ask a follow-up question instead of sharpening it myself.
3. For each belief, write the shift in your words: what changed, and when you noticed it. No shift, no urgency; the belief is marked "timeless" and kept.
4. Write what you think others get wrong as a practice ("hiring before the role is clear"), never a person, a firm or your former employer.
5. Ask for one piece of your own work per belief, from your inventory, in wording you may use in public. No evidence means "not ready to share", not cut.
6. Ask for the strongest counterargument to each belief and write it fairly.
7. Separate your view from your former employer's: mark any belief that only repeats the old firm's line, and ask whether you still hold it now that nobody pays you to.

## Output Format
```markdown
# Point of View Statement
## Belief 1
- Thesis (arguable, my words): [belief]
- The shift (what changed, when I noticed): [shift, or "timeless"]
- What others get wrong (a practice, not a person): [practice]
- Evidence from my own work: [proof number from my inventory, public wording]
- Where I might be wrong: [strongest counterargument]
- Status: [ready to share / not ready: no evidence yet]
## Beliefs 2 and 3
[same fields for each]
## Views I held because my employer did
- [line]: [keep / drop / rethink]
## Decision
[Your name] confirms which beliefs are ready to share and which need evidence first, by [date].
```

## Done When
- Every thesis came from your answers, and a peer could disagree with it.
- Each belief has a shift, or is marked timeless.
- Each belief has evidence from your own work, or is marked not ready.
- Each belief has a fairly stated counterargument.

## Quality Bar
- I ask follow-up questions instead of improving a belief into something you did not say.
- Your wording survives; edits only make a sentence arguable, and you approve them.
- Three beliefs at most; ten beliefs is a list.
- Argue with practices and ideas, never with named people or firms.
- The beliefs are yours, in your words; Claude asks and records, and never supplies an opinion.
