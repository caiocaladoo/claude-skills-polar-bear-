# Early Warning Note

## When To Use
A target will be missed and you are tempted to wait for the monthly report. You are in the building two days a week; by the time the report lands, the cheap fix is gone and the CEO learns it from someone else. Use this the day you know, to say it once, plainly, with a way forward.

## When Not To Use
If nothing has turned and this is the routine week, run Weekly CEO Update. If the problem is a broken commitment between you and a colleague, run Relationship Repair Plan.

## Inputs
- What changed, and how you know: the figure, message or event, with its source and date
- The commitment it touches in the 90-day plan, the statement of work or the board page
- The options you see and what each would cost in time, money or scope
If you have none of this, I start from what you saw and when, mark everything else "unknown, to find out by [date]" and mark the note as a first draft.

## Approach
SBAR (situation, background, assessment, recommendation), the communication tool published by the Institute for Healthcare Improvement and developed for urgent handovers between clinicians. Its order is the method: the problem in one sentence, only the background that matters, what you think, what you want. It suits a warning and is too clinical for a routine update. The failure this prevents is the softened notice, where the turn is buried under context and reassurance and the reader finishes it without knowing a decision is needed.

## Workflow
1. Ask at most three questions: what changed and when did you learn it; which commitment does it touch; what do you need from the CEO, and by when?
2. Situation: one sentence. What changed, on which measure or date. No lead-in and no apology.
3. Background: the commitment it touches and the evidence, with source and date. Only what the reader needs to judge it.
4. Assessment: sort what you have into known, estimated and unknown, and say what it means for the plan. An estimate is labelled; an unknown gets a date by which you will know.
5. Recommendation: two or three options, each with its cost and what it protects, including holding course. State the one you recommend and the decision or help you need, with a date. Promise nothing on behalf of another person or team.
6. Check the tone against one test: could the CEO read only the first two lines and know that something turned and a decision is due? You send it, or say it and follow in writing.

## Output Format
```markdown
# Early Warning Note
Client: [name] · To: [CEO] · Date: [date] · Decision needed by: [date]
## Situation
[One sentence: what changed]
## Background
Commitment it touches: [plan, statement of work or board line] · Evidence: [figure, message, event; source, date]
## Assessment
| Known | Estimated | Unknown, and when I will know |
|---|---|---|
| [fact] | [estimate, labelled] | [gap, date] |
Implication for the plan: [what moves, by how much if known]
## Recommendation
| Option | Cost | What it protects |
|---|---|---|
| [hold course / option / option] | [time, money, scope] | [commitment] |
I recommend: [option] · I need from you: [decision or help]
## Decision
[CEO] chooses an option by [date]; [you] send or say this today and update the plan once it is decided.
```

## Done When
- The first sentence states what changed
- Known, estimated and unknown are in separate columns
- There are two or three options, each with a cost, and one is recommended
- The ask has a date

## Quality Bar
- Sent the week it is true, not held for the next report
- No reassurance that the evidence does not support
- The note describes the event and its effect, never who is to blame
- No commitment is made for another person or team
- Nothing is estimated by Claude or drawn from another client's situation; the evidence is yours, and you deliver the warning yourself
