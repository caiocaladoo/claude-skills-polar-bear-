# Good-Better-Best Engagement Options

## When To Use
You sent one monthly figure and got a yes or a no, mostly a no. Use this once the outcome is confirmed, to give the CEO a choice between three shapes of the seat you would each be glad to take on, instead of one figure they can only push down.

## When Not To Use
If you are still deciding how the seat is charged, run Fractional Pricing Models after this. If the retainer already exists and no longer fits, run Retainer Redesign. If only one honest shape exists, offer one and say so.

## Inputs
- The answer grid from the scoping call: the outcome, what it is worth to them, the alternatives, who decides what
- Your own fees or day rate for comparable work; I never set a price
- What you would and would not take on, and how many days you can really give (your capacity plan if you have one)
If you have none of this, I start from the outcome in your words, leave every price as [your fee] and mark the output as a first draft.

## Approach
Tiered options are a practitioner convention: three offers that differ in what the company gets. The trap is the decoy, described by Huber, Payne and Puto in the Journal of Consumer Research (1982): an option beaten on every count by its neighbour, added only to make that neighbour look good. A CEO feels it, and it costs trust at the moment you need it. For a fractional seat the options must climb by outcome owned and decision rights; three versions of the same job at one, two and three days a week are one option with a discount.

## Workflow
1. Ask at most three questions: which outcome matters most to the CEO, in their words; what is the smallest shape of the seat that still moves it; how many days can you truly give this client?
2. Name the axis the options climb: the outcome owned (advise on it, lead it, run the function), the days and how they fall, and the decision rights that come with each. A typical ladder is advisory, a fractional seat, interim cover; use it only if it fits.
3. Draft the three. For each, one sentence on what it does that the one below does not. If you cannot write it, merge them.
4. Leave each price as [your fee] unless you give me the figure. If you ask what the market pays, I will say I do not know, and ask for the fee you would defend in the room.
5. Write what each option leaves out and the assumptions it rests on: access, a named owner on their side for execution, response times, the CEO's time each week. These are what protect you in month three.
6. Run the decoy check. Compare every pair on outcome, days, the client's effort and fee. If one option loses on every count, remove it or redesign it until someone would rationally choose it.
7. Mark your recommendation and the reason, tied to what the CEO said matters most. The CEO chooses; you decide what you put in front of them.

## Done When
- Each option differs in outcome owned or decision rights, and the difference fits in one sentence
- Every fee is yours or reads [your fee]
- Every pair has passed the decoy check
- You have said you would take on each option gladly, within the days you really have

## Quality Bar
- Options climb by what the company gets, never by the same work with more days
- The smallest option still moves something real; it is not a punishment for a small budget
- A shape where their team does part of the work with its own AI is offered honestly where you offer it
- Assumptions are specific enough for the CEO to check
- Every fee is yours, no option is a decoy, and nothing is borrowed from another client's terms
