# Fractional Positioning Statement

## When To Use
People like you and still cannot say what a fractional leader in your function does. A contact hears that a company needs help, and your name does not come up, because "fractional" told them how you work and nothing about what changes when you arrive. This answers one question: compared with everything else the company could do, why this seat, in a line someone else can repeat?

## When Not To Use
If one CEO is weighing the seat against a full-time hire right now, run Fractional or Full-Time Brief; that page is neutral and belongs to them, this one is yours. If a CEO has just said AI covers half the work, run AI Objection Answer.

## Inputs
- Two or three past engagements you can describe: what was wrong, what you did, what changed. Remove client names unless the work is public
- What those companies did before you or considered instead: a full-time hire, an agency, an adviser, nothing, doing it in house with AI
- How a past client would describe what you do, in their words if you have them
If you have none of this, I start from your current one-line description and one engagement, and mark the output as a first draft.

## Approach
The five components of positioning, as April Dunford sets them out in her public guide: competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, value, the customers who care most, and the market category. The order is the method. You start from what the company would do if you did not exist, because that is what the CEO compares you with, and today that list includes "we will do it ourselves with AI". The failure this prevents is a statement about how you work ("senior, flexible, part time") that never says what the company gets, so nobody can pass it on.

## Workflow
1. Ask at most three questions, all at once: which function and which moment in a company's life do you step into; what did your last clients try or consider before you; which engagement are you proudest of, and why?
2. List the alternatives fairly: a full-time hire, an agency, an adviser, doing nothing, doing it in house with AI. For each, write what it does well before where it falls short. A statement that sneers at any of them does not survive the first meeting.
3. Name the capabilities you bring that those alternatives lack: a decision owner in the room, experience of this exact situation, accountability for a result. Attach proof from your own engagements to each, or mark it "needs proof".
4. Turn each capability into value in the company's terms: what gets decided, built or stopped because of it. Drop any capability you cannot connect to value.
5. Say who cares most: the situation and the role of the person who feels it. Then pick the category frame. The function plus the part-time seat usually beats a new label nobody searches for.
6. Write the line a contact could repeat, with two variants. Read each aloud; if it needs a breath or a buzzword, cut it. You pick one, and I list the proof you still need to write down.

## Done When
- "In house with AI" and "a full-time hire" are both listed and treated fairly
- Every capability has proof from your own work or is marked "needs proof"
- The line says what the company gets, has no jargon and fits in one breath
- No client is named unless you told me the work is public

## Quality Bar
- Alternatives are described honestly, including what they do well; the statement never sells against AI
- Value is in the company's terms, never a list of your methods or your years of experience
- No market claim, benchmark or client result that you did not give me
- Variants are offered and you choose; I do not decide how you describe yourself
- Proof comes from what you give me; nothing is invented, and detail from one client's engagement is never used to describe another's
