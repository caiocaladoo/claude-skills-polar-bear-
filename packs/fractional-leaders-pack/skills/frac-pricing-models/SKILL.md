# Fractional Pricing Models

## When To Use
You price by the hour and every faster week earns you less. Or the CEO offers equity and you do not know what to compare it with. Use this before the fee call to choose the model that fits this seat, and to explain it by what the company buys, not by the time you spend.

## When Not To Use
If you have not yet shaped what you offer, run Good-Better-Best Engagement Options first. If the question is one existing retainer that no longer fits, run Retainer Redesign. This skill chooses the model; it never sets or suggests a number.

## Inputs
- The option or options you are pricing, with outcome, days and decision rights
- How you charge today, and where your own use of AI has made the work faster
- What the CEO has said about budget, equity or paying for results, in their words
If you have none of this, I start from a one-line description of the seat and mark the comparison as a first draft.

## Approach
Fee models fall into three kinds: input (you buy my time), output (you buy deliverables) and outcome (you buy a result), plus hybrids. The writing principle is the US Federal Acquisition Regulation 37.602: describe the work by required results and measurable standards, not by hours. The fractional variants (retainer, day rate, equity, hybrid) follow a public playbook for fractional executives. The failure this prevents is staying on hours because it feels safe while every gain in speed lowers what you earn, or moving to an outcome fee on a result nobody can measure.

## Workflow
1. Ask at most three questions: what does the company get at the end of a quarter with you; how much of that work does AI already speed up; has the CEO raised equity, a success fee or an hourly cap?
2. Write what the client buys as a result with a standard someone could check: "a forecast the board accepts each month", not "two days a week". Every model below is judged against this line.
3. Compare six models on four counts: what the client pays for, who carries the overrun, what AI speed does to it, and the overflow rule it needs. Hourly falls as you get faster; a day-based retainer pays for presence and judgment but needs a rule for asks beyond the days; a scoped project lets you keep the gain and carry the overrun; an outcome-linked fee needs a measure both sides can audit.
4. Test the outcome-linked and hybrid models honestly: is there a measure, a baseline and a source both sides trust? If not, mark it "not possible yet: needs [measure]".
5. Treat equity as a legal and tax matter. I list the questions (vesting, what happens at exit, valuation, tax); check with a qualified adviser.
6. You pick the model, or a mix by phase, and write the reason in two sentences the CEO could read. I state trade-offs; every rate and fee stays yours.

## Done When
- What the client buys is written as a result with a checkable standard
- All six models are compared on the same four counts
- The outcome-linked row names its measure or reads "not possible yet"
- The chosen model has its reason and its overflow rule written down

## Quality Bar
- The comparison talks about what the company gets, never about how busy you are
- AI speed is named plainly, for and against you; nothing hides your own use of AI
- No market rate, benchmark, typical retainer or equity range appears anywhere
- Equity, tax and contract points carry "check with a qualified adviser"
- Claude lays the models side by side from what you give it; you choose and you set every number
