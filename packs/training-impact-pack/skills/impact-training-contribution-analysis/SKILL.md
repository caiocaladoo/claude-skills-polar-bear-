# Training Contribution Analysis

## When To Use
The number moved and someone will ask how you know it was the programme. You have figures, survey answers and perhaps an observation tally, no clean experiment, and you need an argument that survives the question instead of a sentence that claims the whole change.

## When Not To Use
Not for stating the figures: that is the Before-and-After Results Table. Not for putting a share and a money value on the contribution (Training ROI Range), and not for testing the sentences of a finished report (Overclaim Check). If nothing changed, there is no contribution to argue.

## Inputs
- The results table (before, after, comparison, what else changed, with sources and vouchers), and the Success Measures Agreement with its critical behaviours, which give the chain its links.
- Whatever else was collected, at group level only: survey summaries, the observation tally, comment themes, cases. And the decision this evidence feeds, with who makes it.
If you have none of this, I start from the result that moved and what you know about the period, and mark the output "no agreement on file: first draft".

## Approach
This is contribution analysis, the approach created by John Mayne, in its six steps: set out the cause and effect question, develop the theory of change and the risks to it, gather existing evidence, assemble and assess the contribution story, seek additional evidence, revise the story. It meets the ROI Institute's question, "how do you know it was the program?", with reasoning where no control group exists. A result comes from a package of factors, never one. The failure it prevents: the report credits the programme, the sponsor remembers the new tool that went live the same month, and every other figure in the report is doubted from then on.

## Workflow
1. Ask three questions at once: which change are we explaining, and from which row of the results table; which decision does this feed (continue, change, stop or scale) and how confident does that decision need you to be; and what else acted on the result in the period, as you and the sponsor see it?
2. Set the question. Not "did the programme cause it?" but "is it reasonable to conclude the programme contributed to [change]?". Then run the plausibility check: given the size of the programme and everything else acting on the result, is an important contribution likely at all? If not, I say so and stop there.
3. Draw the chain link by link: programme, learning, behaviour on the job, leading indicator, result. Under each link, write the assumption that has to hold for it to lead to the next, such as "people had occasions to use it" or "managers ran the required drivers".
4. Place the existing evidence on each link, from the results table, the surveys, the observation tally and the cases, and mark the link strong or weak. Self-report on its own makes a weak link. A link with no evidence is a gap, and a chain is only as good as its weakest link.
5. List the other explanations: yours, the sponsor's, and any the chain's own assumptions raise. For each, the evidence for it and the evidence against it, treated as seriously as the programme. "Staffing change" and similar factors are about the group, never about named people.
6. Name the extra evidence that would most strengthen or break the story, and who (role) could get it. You decide whether it is worth collecting before the report goes out.
7. Write the contribution story in a few plain sentences, then mark every claim in it shown, suggested or unknown. "Shown" needs a before and an after figure from a named source, a comparison or another named isolation method, and a voucher.

## Done When
- The question is phrased as contribution, with the confidence the decision needs, and every link has its assumption, its evidence or a named gap, a strong or weak mark, and the evidence that would test it.
- Each other explanation has evidence for and against, or "none found".
- Every claim in the story carries one of the three strengths and what it rests on.

## Quality Bar
- The other explanations are argued as well as the programme's case. One that cannot be ruled out stays on the page.
- No share, percentage or money value is put on the contribution here, and no figure appears that is not in your evidence. A weak link is not strengthened by confident wording, and a plausible story is not proof. If the plausibility check fails, the output is short and says so.
- The argument is about the programme and the conditions around it, never about how a named person, manager or trainer performed.
- No cause is claimed that the evidence does not support: every claim is marked shown, suggested or unknown, and other explanations are written down, not left out.
