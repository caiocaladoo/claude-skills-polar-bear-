# Difficult Moments Plan

## When To Use
The room goes flat at 2pm and you are deciding what to do while forty people watch. Use this the week before, when the plan exists and you can still think slowly about what you will do when it stops working. It answers: for each likely wobble, what is my move, what do I say, and what do I cut to pay for it?

## When Not To Use
Not for the minute-by-minute flow: Workshop Run Sheet holds one wobble line per row and points here for the full plan. Not for a tool failing in a virtual room: Virtual Session Redesign owns plan B per tool. And it is not a live aid: you read it before the session and at the break, never from a screen while the room waits.

## Inputs
- The session plan or agenda with its blocks and times, and which blocks carry the practice and the close.
- The group and the conditions (roles, how many, whether they chose to come, who senior is in the room, in person or virtual) and what worries you about this one, in your own words.

If you have none of this, I start from the session type and the group's roles and mark the output as a first draft.

## Approach
This is what-if planning as experienced facilitators do it: decide the move while you are calm. Tannenbaum and Cerasoli's 2013 meta-analysis of debriefs found that structure appears to matter, and the same logic runs here: a move that changes the format beats a clever remark. Edmondson's 1999 study of team psychological safety describes a condition of a team over time; one session cannot install it, so the plan only lowers the cost of speaking today. The failure this prevents: answering a silence by talking more, and louder, to the same silence.

## Workflow
1. Ask up to three questions: which moments you most expect in this room, which blocks must be protected whatever happens, and what you already know about how people came to be there.
2. Lay out one row per situation, the eight in the template plus any you add. Describe the situation as something that happens in a room, never as a person and never as a kind of participant.
3. For each, write two or three readings of what it may mean, all about the design or the conditions: the question was too big, the task was unclear, people do not yet know what is done with their answers.
4. Choose the move, and make it a change of structure: silence becomes pairs then share; one voice becomes a written round; open resistance becomes "what would make this worth your time?" put to the whole room. Name "people who were sent" early and plainly, with a choice about how to use the time.
5. Write the sentence to say, short enough to remember without notes and in words you would use.
6. Decide the cost now, not at 3pm: which block shrinks for each move, and which are protected. The practice and the close are the last to go.
7. Add the line on what is not yours to fix in the room: a conduct issue or a disclosure goes to the right human conversation afterwards.

## Output Format
```markdown

| [situation you add] | [readings] | [move] | [sentence] | [block] |

Order of cuts: [first block to shrink, and the one thing kept from it], then [second block, and what is kept].

[What goes to a conversation afterwards, with whom, and who starts it.]
## Decision
[Trainer name] confirms the protected blocks and the order of cuts with [sponsor or co-trainer name] by [date]; in the room, [trainer name] alone decides whether to use any move.
```

## Done When
- All eight situations have a reading, a move, a sentence and a block that shrinks.
- No row names a person or a type of participant, and every move is a change of format you could start in one sentence.
- The protected blocks and the order of cuts are written down before the day.
- The plan says what is handed to a conversation after the session.

## Quality Bar
- Readings are about the design and the conditions, so the move fixes the session, not somebody.
- Sentences sound like you: read each aloud once and rewrite any you would not say.
- The plan promises no safety and no outcome; it lowers the cost of speaking (pairs first, written first, passing allowed).
- The plan describes situations, never a named person or a kind of participant; in the room you decide whether to use any of it.
