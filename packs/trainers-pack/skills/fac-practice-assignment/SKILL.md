# Practice Assignment
## When To Use
People practised in the room, and the first real attempt is left to chance. Use this before the session ends, or in the days right after, to offer everyone one task that is already on their desk and that cannot be done without the skill. It answers one question: where, in work people already have, does the first real try happen, and who do they talk it over with?

## When Not To Use
If each person should choose their own commitment, run If-Then Action Plan; this is one task you offer to the whole group. If the skill needs several assignments, coaching and sessions over months, run 70-20-10 Programme Plan. And if participants have no occasion to use the skill in the coming weeks, no task will fix that: say so to the sponsor.

## Inputs
- The session's learning points and what people practised in the room.
- The work participants have coming up, by role: the meetings, conversations, documents or decisions where the skill belongs.
- When the group meets again, if it does: a next session, a check-in call, or nothing.
If you have none of this, I start from the session title and one situation where the skill is used, and mark the output as a first draft.

## Approach
Grossman and Salas's 2011 review of training transfer lists opportunity to perform among the work-environment factors most consistently linked to transfer. The review describes a link, and this task promises no result; it only stops the first attempt depending on luck. The failure it prevents is homework: an extra exercise, a reflection sheet, a "try it and let me know", which the people with the fullest calendars skip, and which then gets read as lack of motivation when it was lack of room.

## Workflow
1. Ask at most three questions: what real work do participants have in the coming weeks that the skill belongs in, how long a window do you want to give them, and where can the experience come back to the group?
2. Pick ONE task that is real work and forces the skill: a conversation they must hold, a meeting they must run, a document they must write. Never an extra exercise and never "reflect on".
3. Set the window as `[days]` you choose, and list what the participant needs ready before they start (the occasion, the handout page, a few minutes to prepare).
4. Write "what good looks like" as three to five observable points taken from the session's learning points. It is for the participant's own use, a mirror and not a marking sheet.
5. Set up the peer partner. Participants choose who they pair with. One short conversation after the attempt: what I tried, what happened, what I would change. In threes it can follow Troika Consulting, from Liberating Structures on its originators' site (shared under a Creative Commons licence): one presents, two ask clarifying questions, then the two discuss while the presenter listens.
6. Plan how it comes back: a slot in the next session or check-in where pairs share what they learned, as themes. A task not done is information about conditions (time, opportunity) and joins the barrier themes in aggregate.
7. Hand over the check you run before offering it: "is this a task they would have done anyway this month?" If the answer is no, it is homework, and you replace it.

## Output Format
```markdown
# Practice Assignment
Session: [title] | Window: [days] after the session | Offered to: [roles]
## The task
[One real task at work that cannot be done without the skill]
What to have ready: [occasion, material, preparation time]
## What good looks like (for your own use)
| Observable point | From learning point |
|---|---|
| [what you would see or hear] | [learning point] |
## Peer partner
Pairs or trios: chosen by participants | When: [within days of the attempt] | Length: [minutes]
1. What I tried
2. What happened
3. What I would change
## How it comes back
| Where | When | What pairs share |
|---|---|---|
| [next session / check-in] | [date] | [themes, no names] |

If the task could not be done: [the condition that stopped it, noted as a theme]
## Decision
[Trainer] decides whether this task is offered as written, and [sponsor or programme owner] confirms the slot where it comes back, by [date].
```

## Done When
- The task is work participants already have, and you have confirmed they would have done it anyway this month.
- The window is a number of days you set, and the task says what to have ready.
- "What good looks like" has three to five points, each something a person could see or hear.
- The way back to the group is a named slot with a date, or the plan says plainly that there is none.

## Quality Bar
- One task. A menu of options is three tasks nobody starts.
- Peer partners are chosen by participants, never assigned by level or role seniority.
- What comes back is themes; no list of who attempted it exists anywhere.
- No efficacy promise: the source reports a link between opportunity and transfer, nothing more.
- The task is offered, never marked; nobody reports who did it or how well.
