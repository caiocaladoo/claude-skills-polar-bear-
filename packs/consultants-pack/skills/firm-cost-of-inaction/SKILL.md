# Cost of Inaction

## When To Use
The conversations are warm and the problem is real, yet your work sits behind nine other priorities because nobody has seen what staying put costs. Run this after a call where the client gave at least a few figures. It answers whether waiting costs them anything they care about, and it is allowed to conclude that it does not.

## When Not To Use
Before the call, with no figures yet, prepare the implication questions in Discovery Call Questions. Once the impact is confirmed, Problem Statement carries the short version into the proposal.

## Inputs
- Your call notes, or a transcript made with everyone's consent, with the client's figures and who gave each
- Their answers on consequences, even partial ones
- Any deadline, event or decision they mentioned
If you have none of this, I build the empty sheet with the questions to ask and mark it as a first draft.

## Approach
Laying out the cost of doing nothing is a consulting habit, described generically: show what the problem costs if nobody acts, so the client weighs acting against waiting and not your fee against zero. It works only with their numbers. Bring an estimate or a benchmark and the client corrects it, then doubts every other line you wrote. So blanks stay blank, and "nothing changes if we wait" is a legitimate finding worth having before you write a proposal.

## Workflow
1. Ask, in one go: which figures did the client give, and who said each one? Did they mention a deadline or an event? Did anybody describe what waiting would mean?
2. One row per consequence, described in the client's words: what it touches and who feels it.
3. Per row, record their figure and unit, who gave it (by role), when, and the period it covers. A figure stays exactly as said; "a couple of days a month" is written as "a couple of days a month" and never turned into money.
4. Where there is no figure the cell stays empty and the question that would fill it goes beside it. I do not estimate, borrow a typical figure or extend a trend.
5. Do arithmetic only on their figures, one visible step at a time (their monthly figure times the months they named), with the rows each result used. No total that mixes units.
6. Record the timing pressure as they stated it, with who said it. Then the wait-a-quarter line: what changes in three months, in their words. If the answer is "not much", write that down and tell you plainly; the timing may be wrong.

## Output Format
```markdown
# Cost of Waiting Sheet
Client: [name] · Based on: [call date; notes or consented transcript]
## Consequences in their figures
| Consequence (their words) | Figure and unit | Said by (role), when | Period | Question if blank |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [consequence] | [as stated, or blank] | [role, date] | [period] | [question] |
## Working
| Result | Calculation | Rows used |
|---|---|---|
| [label] | [their figure] x [their period] = [result] | [n, n] |
## Timing pressure
- [Deadline, event or decision as they stated it] (said by [role])
## If they wait a quarter
[What changes, in their words, or "nothing changes"]
## Decision
[You] decide by [date] whether the cost supports proposing now or coming back later; [client role] confirms each figure before it appears in any document.
```

## Done When
- Each figure has a role and a date; each blank has its question
- Every calculation shows its steps and uses client figures only
- The wait-a-quarter answer is written down, including when it is "nothing"

## Quality Bar
- Units stay as stated; nothing is converted into money or time the client did not give
- No benchmark, sector figure or typical cost anywhere on the sheet
- A weak cost is reported as weak, never inflated to create urgency
- The sheet is for understanding; no pressure line is built on it
- Every number is the client's, with who said it; I add none, and you decide whether the case is strong enough to propose.
